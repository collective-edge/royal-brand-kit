Color for data

Royal Ambulance · the set you use for charts, dashboards and reported numbers

Chart colors are a separate set, and they are the same in every one of our brands. A chart made for Royal Ambulance reads the same inside an Apex Paramedics or a Collective Edge document. No chart color is a brand color, and no brand color is a chart color.

The one rule. When there is data on the page, color has a job: separate things, and signal things. The brand is carried by the band at the top, the logo and the typeface. Not by the bars.

01Series colors

For charts with more than one line, bar group or slice. Use them in this order, starting at the first. Do not pick the ones you like and do not reorder them. The order is what keeps neighboring colors apart, including for the roughly one man in twelve who cannot separate red from green.

None of the seven is green or red. Green and red belong to the direction colors below. Seven is the limit. If you need an eighth, group the smallest into “Other” or split the chart. On scatter plots, bubble charts and maps, where any two marks can end up touching, the limit is four on white and three on a dark band.

02Direction colors

Separate from the series colors, and never mixed with them. These tell the reader how to feel about a number. They are the alert colors, used on a number.

Green does not mean the number went up. Green means the news is good.

In one of our reports, transports rose 6.3 percent and hospital invoice costs fell 38.8 percent. One went up, one went down, and both were good news. Both are green. The arrow shows which way the number moved. The color shows whether that is good.

Coloring a 38.8 percent cost saving red because it has a minus sign tells the reader we made things worse.

Always pair the color with an arrow or icon and a word. Never color alone. Decks get printed in black and white, projected onto washed-out screens, and read by people who cannot see the difference between your red and your green.

03The before-and-after chart

The most common chart we make, and the one most often got wrong. Do not use a light shade of one color for “before” and a dark shade of it for “after”. They are too close, so the reader has to stop and read the labels. Use a plain gray baseline against the first series color.

04Above and below a line

For a value that sits over or under a baseline: budget variance, days ahead or behind. Red below, blue above, gray in the middle. Not red and green.

05The ramp, for “how much”

For heatmaps and intensity grids, where one quantity gets darker as it gets bigger. This is the one data color that follows the brand: each brand has its own ramp, and it ends on that brand’s color. Use the ramp of the brand whose document it is. Steps 100 to 700, light to dark.

Never use a ramp for things that merely have names. Facilities, payers and crews are not more or less of anything, and shading them light to dark invents a ranking that is not there. Use the series colors.

06Quick rules

Use

  • Make every color do one job.
  • Use series colors in order, from slot 1.
  • Pair every direction color with an arrow and a word.
  • Use a gray baseline for before-and-after.
  • Keep green for good news.

Avoid

  • Use a brand color as a chart color or a status color.
  • Use two shades of one color for two different things.
  • Use red and green as the ends of one scale. Use red and blue.
  • Color a number red when the news is good.
  • Shade facility names light to dark.
  • Invent an eighth series color.
