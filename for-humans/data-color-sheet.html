Color for data

Royal Ambulance · the set you use for charts, dashboards and reported numbers

The brand palette is not what you color a chart with. The purple palette is for the band, the logo and the type. These colors are for the data. They are the same across Royal Ambulance, Apex Paramedics and Collective Edge, so a chart means the same thing in any of our documents.

Why there is a separate set. The old rule asked for four shades of brand purple on a chart. Measured against the standard test for whether two colors can be told apart, two of those four scored 6.6 where the passing mark is 15. Not difficult for color blind readers. Difficult for everyone.

Series colors

For charts with more than one line, bar group or slice. Use them in this order, starting at the first. The order is what keeps neighboring colors apart, including for the roughly one man in twelve who cannot separate red from green. Do not reorder them to suit a deck.

Three is the limit on scatter plots, bubble charts and maps, where any two marks can end up touching. Eight everywhere else. If you need a ninth, group the smallest into “Other” or split the chart. Amber and orange are light, so put the number directly on those bars.

Separate from the series colors, and never mixed with them. These tell the reader how to feel about a number.

Green does not mean the number went up. Green means the news is good.

In one of our reports, transports rose 6.3 percent and hospital invoice costs fell 38.8 percent. One went up, one went down, and both were good news. Both are green. The arrow shows which way the number moved; the color shows whether that is good.

Coloring a 38.8 percent cost saving red because it has a minus sign tells the reader we made things worse.

Always pair the color with an arrow or icon and a word. Never color alone. Decks get printed in black and white, projected onto washed-out screens, and read by people who cannot see the difference between your red and your green.

The before-and-after chart

The most common chart we make, and the one most often got wrong. Do not use a light purple for "before" and a dark purple for "after". They are too close, so the reader has to stop and read the labels. Use a plain gray baseline against the brand purple.

The purple ramp, for "how much"

For heatmaps and intensity grids, where one quantity gets darker as it gets bigger. It finishes on Royal Purple, so the brand color is the darkest step.

100200300400500600700

Start at 300 for anything with distinct steps, like funnel stages. The first two are only usable on a smooth gradient. Never use this ramp for things that merely have names. Facilities and payers are not more or less of anything, and shading them light to dark invents a ranking that is not there.

Quick rules

Do

  • Make every color do one job.
  • Use series colors in order, from slot 1.
  • Pair every direction color with an arrow and a word.
  • Use a gray baseline for before-and-after.
  • Label amber and orange series directly.

Do not

  • Use two shades of purple for two different things.
  • Use red and green as the ends of one scale. Use red and teal.
  • Color a number red when the news is good.
  • Shade facility names light to dark.
  • Invent a ninth series color.
